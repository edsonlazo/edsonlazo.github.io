If you work as a developer in LATAM, you have probably thought about working for a company abroad at some point, and maybe you also thought you weren't ready. I thought the same. Today I've been at Deel for almost 4 years, and I want to write about the steps I followed to get this job.

I'm not going to write about the specific interview process I went through.

## Contents

## English

This was the most important one. While there are opportunities at Latin American companies that don't require English, the best ones do. Knowing English can massively increase the number of openings you can apply to.

Personally, I had the privilege of learning English through courses when I was younger. Even so, I think it's something you can learn as an adult, and it's worth investing in.

To practice, I used a Google tool called Interview Warmup. Unfortunately, Google seems to have killed it (like [many of its other projects](https://killedbygoogle.com/)). Still, these days most AI tools can help you practice, like Gemini Live or ChatGPT's voice mode.

## I polished my LinkedIn

My LinkedIn was super neglected: it had the places I'd worked at and little else. LinkedIn is a tool recruiters use a lot, so it never hurts to give it some love and keep it up to date. It's also important to do what you can to be easy to find in search.

What specific changes did I make?

1. **I updated my headline so it had keywords.** In my case I wrote:

   > Software Engineer | JavaScript | Node.js | TypeScript | Backend lover

   This way, recruiters could find me if they searched for engineers with JavaScript knowledge or who specialized in backend.

2. **I filled in the "About" section.** I wrote a short description of myself using the same keywords and added my experience. LinkedIn rewards complete profiles, so it's good to have this section filled in.

3. **I updated my work experience.** To be honest, this was the most tedious part. For each position I had, I added 3 bullet points following the formula **action verb + quantitative impact**. Basically: "I did X, which achieved Y results". For example:

   > Reduced the time it took to build new integrations by 50% by building a services gateway to automate the work.

## I updated my CV

Before this push to get a job, my CV was basically a Canva template with information that I now realize is completely irrelevant to a recruiter (or to an automated candidate screening system): hobbies, or little bars showing my level of expertise with each tool.

![Little bars showing the level of expertise in different skills on my old CV](/assets/barritas.png)

_What do these little bars even mean? How do you measure someone's ability to manage large teams like this?_ 🤷

[The CV I submitted](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1ol9NKTmNH2A1ijuFsnrkB9AF_SfvAwaR1-XZn5W7daA/edit?usp=sharing) for this job is much more minimalist and follows practically the same advice as LinkedIn: list each experience with at least 3 bullet points following the formula.

## I practiced interviews

I still remember the first time I was asked "what's your biggest weakness?", in one of my first interviews when I was starting my career. I was mortified and couldn't answer. Not because I thought I had no weaknesses, but because I couldn't think of what to say without hurting my chances of getting the job.

After that experience I promised myself it wouldn't happen again. I looked up the most common questions, and prepared and practiced answers for them. There are well-known methods for answering this kind of question, like the [STAR method](https://capd.mit.edu/resources/the-star-method-for-behavioral-interviews/). What matters is that you take the time to practice, so when the moment comes you reduce the chance of going blank.

By the way, these days I say my biggest weakness is being messy, but that I'm working on it with different tools: a schedule for tidying up, checklists, etc. The point of the question is to show how you're overcoming that weakness.

## I practiced coding exercises and theory

This is more focused on my work as a software engineer. I started solving algorithm problems in JavaScript: at the time I paid for [AlgoExpert](https://algoexpert.io), which I felt was quite complete, and I complemented it with LeetCode exercises. I also reviewed JavaScript and Node.js concepts.

Honestly, I feel this was the step with the least impact, because none of the processes asked me to do live coding.

## Letting them reject me

This was probably the hardest part. When I started looking for jobs at companies outside Bolivia, it went pretty badly with the first ones I applied to. I usually did well in the non-technical interviews, but I failed the technical ones many times (mainly because at first I hadn't reviewed theory).

After those first rejections, it would have been easy to start doubting myself and stop applying so my ego wouldn't keep taking hits. I had a hard time, I won't deny it, but I always let the company be the one to tell me no. I decided not to tell myself no, and I kept applying even to companies I didn't think would give me a chance.

To be honest, in Deel's case it was a recruiter who reached out to me, not me who applied. Seeing the size of the company I didn't feel ready to go through the process, but in the end I decided to do it and, thanks to my effort and the experience from previous interviews, I got the job.

## Conclusion

If I had to sum it all up: improve your English, update your LinkedIn and your CV with keywords and measurable impact, practice the typical interview questions and, above all, let the companies be the ones to tell you no.

If you're hesitating to apply for that job you feel is out of your league, apply anyway. The worst that can happen is a rejection, and every rejection leaves you better prepared for the next process.

_Fortis fortuna adiuvat_: fortune favors the brave.
